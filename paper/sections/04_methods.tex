\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

This section reviews methods branch by branch. We report each study's own numbers, under its own protocol. Where independent work has qualified a result, we place the qualification next to it rather than deferring it to \cref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{B1: Reading weights}
\label{sec:read}

\paragraph{The original reading results.} In the founding post, the editor model named which Atari-style game each of four PPO policies (17{,}253 weights each) was trained on, without being given the options. It identified the shape generated by small diffusion models and the language of character-level Transformers from their middle blocks. It failed to tell a sycophantic Gemma~3 270M fine-tune from an honest one using weights alone, and succeeded only when given attention patterns \citep{takehi2026sparks}.

\paragraph{Controlled reading studies.} The follow-up literature quickly turned to asking \emph{what} the reader uses.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Familiar labels.} Ahmed permuted output labels and withheld dataset names for small CNNs. The reader still found the class split at the new indices, but named every class pair wrong. It also called networks trained on KMNIST ``MNIST'' and networks trained on food photos ``CIFAR-10'' \citep{ax2611_08070}. The study concludes that zero-shot reading is better described as ``local arithmetic plus dataset priors than as weight-flow tracing'' \citep{ax2611_08070}.
\item \textbf{Training footprints.} An AI-authored two-model replication found that row lengths, a training footprint, drove game naming. When row lengths and weights pointed to different games, both readers followed the row lengths, although when row lengths were disguised one reader still named every game correctly. On randomized-weight controls, one reader declined to name a game in 19 of 20 attempts while the other named one in all 20 \citep{zen24373915}.
\item \textbf{Function-preserving changes.} Cha et al.\ showed that learned and LLM weight readers change their answers under hidden-unit permutations and positive rescalings that leave the network's function unchanged. GPT-6 Astra's accuracy fell from 88.3\% to 81.1\% under such equivalent reparameterizations, although the study's exact tests do not establish that the drop is significant \citep{ax2611_05798}.
\item \textbf{Size and tools.} Liu and Yang found that tool-free reading recovered planted relationships in 353-weight networks but became unreliable at 3{,}457 weights. They also found that a calculator with only black-box query access matched white-box access, so the useful signal came from computation, not weight access \citep{ax2611_07847}. A companion study audited 32 tabular clinical models through queries alone. The LLM found the planted flaw in 69 of 72 runs, while a purpose-written audit script found all 24 planted flaws \citep{ax2611_24877}.
\item \textbf{Exactly checkable targets.} On recurrent game policies of 1{,}218 weights, where every answer can be scored exactly, a mid-size open model recovered the implemented state machine in 62 of 66 replies and gave an exact behavior table in 34 of 36. Writing succeeded more often when the model emitted a state machine for compilation (20 of 20) than when it wrote weights directly (13 of 20). Repair was weak: 4 of 18 networks repaired \citep{ax2611_31979}. The study is one model with three replicates per cell.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Auditing and backdoor detection.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Clinical networks with planted flaws.} Nusrat and Nusrat planted flaws in clinical screening networks with 433 to 4{,}801 parameters. The LLM named the broken rule in 16 of 18 flawed networks. But it cleared only 2 of 5 healthy networks without being forced, and found 1 of 3 triggers. A column-norm statistic with no language model identified the hidden rule in all 20 confound networks \citep{ax2611_05341}.
\item \textbf{A preregistered genomics test.} In a preregistered, blinded test on a genomics CNN, detection was supported only narrowly, and every correct detection came from sessions that ran parts of the network locally \citep{zen24040591}. A ``no forward pass'' condition can therefore be bypassed by an agent that evaluates layers itself.
\item \textbf{Smaller readers.} 1.5B and 7B open models scored at chance, and no better than a value-shuffled control, on five weight-reading tasks \citep{zen24047726}.
\item \textbf{Frontier auditors against a closed-form implant.} Lazarus plants agent backdoors by editing fewer than ten dormant neurons in closed form. The construction is not LLM-authored at experiment time, although the authors credit an AI coding agent with discovering its core idea. Given raw checkpoints and two hours each, GPT-6 Astra flagged 72.81\% of edited checkpoints and abstained on the rest, while Claude Opus 5.5 flagged 56.14\% and called 41.23\% of edited checkpoints clean \citep{ax2611_15118}.
\item \textbf{A non-LLM reader.} Khilar showed that a backdoor trigger token can be read from fine-tune weight deltas by a closed-form statistic. It recovered the exact trigger in 14 of 14 ordinary backdoors on two sub-0.5B models, but was defeated by an attacker that freezes and fills the scanned block \citep{ax2611_19304}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A trained-reader precursor.} Weight Oracles, posted four weeks before the term was coined, fine-tuned a 3B language model to read the raw weights of toy Transformers of about 2K parameters. The weights were injected as learned continuous embeddings. The model simulated the target's forward pass with 99.0\% test accuracy on held-out models, and flagged unseen backdoors zero-shot with an AUROC of 0.93 on one cohort, and 0.81 on a composite measure \citep{ax2610_07334}. It differs from WSP in that the reader is trained for the job. It is the natural baseline for asking how much a general-purpose reader gains from that training.

\subsection{B2: Writing networks from a specification}
\label{sec:write}

\paragraph{Games and control.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The original post.} The editor wrote zero-initialized MinAtar policies, with no data, gradients or rollouts, that outscored PPO trained for 100 million steps in all three games. It also wrote a 1.48M-weight 9$\times$9 Go network \citep{takehi2026sparks}.
\item \textbf{Replications.} An independent replication obtained Breakout scores of 514, 550 and 307 across three runs, in line with the original 517. It then decompiled the written networks exactly into programs \citep{ax2611_21449}.
\item \textbf{Exhaustive correctness checks.} A difficulty-graded ladder of ten two-player games found that six of the ten hand-set networks are correct under exhaustive search against independently written oracles \citep{ax2611_09271}.
\item \textbf{Written weights versus Python players.} The largest controlled writing study compared several writer models on private games. GPT-6 Astra's written actors solved 40\% of held-out episodes one-shot and 56\% with feedback. Every writer's Python player beat its own written weights, by 39 to 75 points one-shot. The written weights were fragile: random noise of $10^{-7}$ of each tensor's RMS already removed much of their success \citep{ax2611_16981}.
\item \textbf{Formally checked policies.} Gross wrote policy networks and checked them with probabilistic model checking (Storm). All 18 Frozen Lake runs reached the optimal satisfaction probability, and the study shows that dataset accuracy is a poor proxy for the formal property \citep{ax2611_11604}.
\item \textbf{A physical machine.} A 253-parameter tractor steering controller written by LLMs was tested on a real machine. The LLMs needed 25 simulated evaluations; an evolution strategy with 1{,}000 found no working controller from zero. Gradient training could not start from exactly zero weights, where every gradient vanishes, but from random weights it reached lower error than every LLM-written controller with far more simulation \citep{ax2611_21958}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scientific and tabular models.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Physics2Weight} writes both architecture and weights for PDE solutions within a 501-parameter cap. It reports relative $L_2$ errors near machine precision on two of four PDEs, where the writer encoded the known closed-form solution, but most of the accuracy gain comes from editing the architecture rather than from the weight values \citep{ax2611_12954}.
\item \textbf{Quantum circuits.} Direct writing of variational-circuit angles matched an optimizer within 0.007 on eleven problems, and failed on deeper fixed ansätze \citep{ax2611_11537}. A second study found success depends strongly on Hamiltonian structure \citep{ax2611_17246}.
\item \textbf{Medical imaging.} LLM-written LeNet-style classifiers reached a mean test AUC of 0.719 across six MedMNIST tasks without training data. They beat a zero-shot vision-language model on 5 of 6 tasks but lost to the same network trained on 256 labels \citep{ax2611_12066}.
\item \textbf{Materials science.} Written networks served as zero-label priors and beat the best label-free physical descriptor on every task, by 0.04 to 0.17 \citep{ax2611_22013}.
\item \textbf{Tabular data.} A tabular foundation model written from zero reached a mean ROC AUC of 0.849 on 20 TabArena datasets, against 0.876 for the pretrained baseline \citep{ax2611_20174}.
\item \textbf{Statistical tasks.} Opus wrote small regressors and classifiers from in-context data. For example, one hand-written network reached $R^2 = 0.9997$, equal to least squares \citep{ax2611_19292}.
\item \textbf{Combinatorial optimization.} In a study whose text and weights were generated by Claude Opus 5.5, LLM-written branching networks matched or beat networks trained by imitation of strong branching in 3 of 4 settings, but SCIP's own pseudocost rule matched the gain on the applied crew-scheduling problem and the written rule solved fewer MIPLIB instances (19 against 24); an LLM-written arc scorer for column generation tied the classical reduced-cost rule \citep{ax2611_09559}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Writing beyond Transformers.} When the writer may choose the architecture, the result looks less like a neural network and more like a program.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item Interpretable WSP writes all weights of small GPT-style models in code against a fixed evaluator. All 26 algorithmic runs reached 100\% visible and hidden accuracy, and the smallest such models had a median of 166 parameters \citep{ax2611_18114}.
\item Given free choice, three writer LLMs compiled plain-text facts into sparse tables read by a fixed procedure rather than writing Transformers \citep{ax2611_22055}. Their written Transformers scored below BM25 over the same written text \citep{ax2611_22055}.
\item Writing in-context learning into weights of a Qwen2.5-0.5B model matched 16-shot ICL on the primary tasks (88.5\% and 90.4\% for two editors, against 89.3\%). When every run was scored as it was left, including unfinished ones, one editor fell below the baselines \citep{ax2611_26841}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pattern.} Across B2, written networks behave like compiled programs: sparse, often integer-valued, and exactly decompilable \citep{ax2611_21449,ax2611_16981}. They are competitive when the rules are known and expressible, and fall behind modest amounts of training data when they are not \citep{ax2611_12066,ax2611_22013}. Their fragility to tiny perturbations \citep{ax2611_16981} is a direct concern for \cref{sec:persist}.

\subsection{B3: Repairing and editing trained networks}
\label{sec:repair}

\paragraph{The Qwen loop-repair family.} The single most replicated WSP result is the original MATH-500 repair of Qwen3.5-0.8B. The editor traced a ``loop'' direction written by the MLPs of layers 15 and 19 and subtracted it from 64 output columns in each, changing 131{,}072 weights. This took MATH-500 from 7.9\% to 48.1\% and cut the share of answers hitting the token limit from 93\% to 33\% \citep{takehi2026sparks}. Independent work has reproduced and qualified this result from several directions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Re-runs.} The released edit rebuilds bit-exactly, and an evidence review reports that one full independent re-run gave 51.0\% \citep{zen23954378}. A low-budget replication with half the forward passes, evaluated on 384 held-out GSM8K and MATH training-split problems at an 8k-token budget, raised accuracy from 9.38\% to 53.65\% (MATH alone: 8.33\% to 41.67\%) \citep{zen24416417}. A Claude-based replication reached 51.4\% to 58.1\%, and found that sampling temperature alone lifts MATH-500 to 22.1\%, about a third of the gain \citep{ax2611_20953}.
\item \textbf{Other editors and benchmarks.} A Mythos replication went from 7.3\% to 32.5\% at a 10-pass budget and 58.7\% after further passes. It added a thinking-disabled control that already scored 42.8\% \citep{zen24036186}. A GSM8K replication found agent edits 6.5 to 9 points above thinking off on locked test sets; its LoRA and no-agent script comparisons, which the edits also beat, were run on the practice set \citep{zen24208534}. A systematic study across seven benchmarks and two scales found gains concentrated at 0.8B, where truncation drives the baseline failure \citep{ax2611_09382}.
\item \textbf{Mechanism and decoding.} The gain disappears in the model's default (non-thinking) mode, where the edit changed accuracy by $0.0 \pm 1.25$ points pooled across four benchmarks \citep{zen24373917}. Sampling settings alone move the published edit between 25\% and 55\% on 40 problems, measured on an 8-bit (q8\_0) copy of the model \citep{zen24058693}, and greedy decoding without a presence penalty reduces it to 4 of 100 sampled problems \citep{zen24235217}. On spatial-reasoning tasks the edit helps by stopping looping, and an element-permuted copy of the same delta does nothing \citep{ax2611_27089}. Tracing executed Python before and after edits separates gains from submitting already-computed values versus changed computation \citep{ax2611_20566}.
\end{itemize}
Taken together, the edit is real and reproducible: it removes a repetition failure. There is no evidence that it adds mathematical ability, which matches the original authors' own finding that a non-looping Gemma model could not be improved \citep{takehi2026sparks}.

\paragraph{Robot policies.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item On the 3.6B $\pi_{0.5}$ vision-language-action policy, three independent tool-assisted, simulator-feedback editing sessions raised success from 86.3\% to 89.4\%, 88.0\% and 92.0\%. The repair was an embedding-row lexical remapping \citep{ax2611_09376}.
\item A closed-form, MEMIT-style update that distills an instruction rewording into the weights raised a DROID policy from 21.1\% to 82.8\%. A matched random-direction update scored 9 of 64, and only 1 of 7 blind Claude Opus editors passed the held-out test \citep{ax2611_11894}.
\item On a 3.35B $\pi_{0.5}$ policy, Claude Opus 5.5 with tools wrote small edits to the action expert that trigger a corrective movement from a recognizable stuck state. Success rose from 56.5\% to 70.5\% on a hammer task (41 scenes fixed, 13 broken) and from 67.7\% to 84.4\% on a policy that had already been fine-tuned for the task. Random edits of the same size made no consistent difference, and a sign-reversed edit increased the targeted failure \citep{ax2611_31715}. Edits for two other tasks gave no significant improvement. Results are single runs in simulation.
\item On a grasping network, the editor correctly diagnosed a confidence ceiling, but removing it did not improve success and the edits were reverted \citep{ax2611_18919}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Vision, speech and science models.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Vision-language models.} Six repair sessions raised spatial accuracy of a damaged SmolVLM from 38.5\% to 83.9\%. The study also identified a confound: faults computed in float32 on BF16 weights leave low-bit traces that the agent exploited \citep{ax2611_23615}.
\item \textbf{Loops in vision-language models.} Keel gives a frontier editor images, a census of looping failures and a repair objective based on loop share rather than accuracy, and selects among several independent editors. Averaged over six benchmarks, Qwen3.5-2B rose from 65.2 to 72.3, against 71.3 for budget forcing, a decoding-only method. On the 0.8B model the Keel edit starts from the original MIT edit, which already scores 55.0 against Keel's 56.1 \citep{ax2611_25257}.
\item \textbf{Few-shot detection heads.} An agent wrote the class-head rows of a frozen detector for new classes. At five shots it beat full-decoder fine-tuning on two of the four main datasets and tied on the other two. A closed-form formula distilled from the agent's sessions scored higher than the agent's own edits (mean F1 47.2 against 41.6), a classifier outside the head beat both, and the advantage over fine-tuning was gone by ten shots \citep{ax2611_28105}.
\item \textbf{Blind diagnosis.} Blind diagnosis and repair of deployment faults in vision models repaired 123 of 162 faulted models and edited none of 21 fault-free ones. On known fault families, a script written after the fact nearly matched the agent (30 versus 33 of 48) \citep{ax2611_28781}.
\item \textbf{Installing capabilities.} WeightCode installed a new command grammar into a ViT by changing 257 weights (exact match 0\% to 99.8\%). Rerunning the editor's program on independently trained networks transferred the gain, while copying the numeric delta did not \citep{ax2611_25476}.
\item \textbf{Distilled recipes.} TowerBridge used agent sessions to discover an edit that closes the modality gap in CLIP models, then distilled it into a closed-form recipe that beats the agent's own patches \citep{ax2611_31233}.
\item \textbf{Diffusion editing.} A preregistered diffusion-editing study found no advantage of the WSP pipeline over strong published closed-form editors \citep{ax2611_22228}.
\item \textbf{Speech recognition.} A speech-recognition replication found that all nine agent edits beat matched random controls, but the failure itself was mostly caused by a missing attention mask \citep{zen23976684}.
\item \textbf{Neuron rewrites in larger LLMs.} An agent with gradients blocked rewrote 8 MLP neurons of Gemma-4-12B with closed-form ridge detectors. This cut a non-preferred translation tense from 26\% to 5\% on held-out items, with translation quality unchanged. The edit beat a system-prompt rule, which broke 49 control items, and a LoRA baseline (12\% and 23\%). A review included in the same record found that doubling one native neuron gives the same shift \citep{zen24156363}.
\item \textbf{Smaller studies.} A particle-physics foundation model was repaired comparably by an agent, by removing sparse-autoencoder latents, and by fine-tuning \citep{ax2611_27079}. Value trade-offs in small classifiers could be edited \citep{ax2611_24330}. A compact TTS model received selective edits that did not beat matched random controls \citep{ax2611_29455}.
\item \textbf{Benchmarks.} IWIBench scores LLM-driven edits of a 4B Gemma model across ten task families. Frontier editors scored 0.29 to 0.46 macro reward and open editors 0.01 to 0.05 \citep{ax2611_08647}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Production recommenders and quantized networks.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item Weight Coding applies agentic editing to a 147M-parameter production two-tower retriever. Opus 5.5 inspects the weights and applies discrete edits (resets, rescales, steering), and some values are then tuned by an evolution-strategy search over parameters the agent selects. Held-out recall@10 rose by up to 15.0 points, in about a quarter of the GPU hours of a fine-tune. The agent's own discrete edits gave gains of up to 10.1 points, and the largest gains (13.7 and 15.0 points) came from edits whose values were tuned by the search. No random-edit or search-only control is reported \citep{ax2611_29613}.
\item Given the complete weights of a 20{,}280-weight CNN as text, with no tools, data or inference, Claude models produced 4-bit versions. Round-to-nearest quantization drops accuracy from 81.4\% to 60.4\%. The best response (Opus 5.5) reached 77.95\%, 2.8 points below AdaRound, and 1{,}314 of its 1{,}364 edits coincide in position and value with a published data-free rule (kernel balancing). The authors state that their runs ``cannot tell whether the rule is derived from the weights or recalled'' \citep{ax2611_33031}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Implanting behavior.} The same operations implant backdoors.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item In an open-loop, no-forward-pass setting, one of five editors reached 100\% attack success on all three targets. On one target it changed six scalar parameters, with clean accuracy at 94.99\% \citep{ax2611_17395}.
\item A closed-loop study with a cheap editor implanted selective backdoors in Qwen and Llama models at \$1.74 per run in API fees. It reached 99.2\% attack success while shifting the average of seven held-out multiple-choice tasks by 0.07 points and GSM8K strict accuracy by 1.69 points \citep{ax2611_27920}.
\item TrojanMaxxing implanted visually triggered backdoors into safety-classifier VLMs by editing 0.0003--0.023\% of weights for \$9 to \$30 per run \citep{ax2611_27425}.
\item Outside WSP proper, a fixed closed-form projection removes all guardrails, or only cyber-related ones, from open-weight models of up to 320B parameters without gradients. The same paper proposes a weight-diff audit that detects such edits when the base checkpoint is available \citep{ax2611_27426}.
\item In a related audit, an agent's rewrite of 8 Gemma-4-12B neurons, and a separately trained implant, were both caught by a reference-free per-neuron norm scan. Norm matching defeated the scan \citep{zen24156363}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{B4: Feedback and search loops}
\label{sec:loops}

Most WSP studies already use feedback: the editor runs forward passes, sees results and revises. B4 collects work where the loop itself is the contribution.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Experimental feedback.} WeightLoop turns experimental feedback into persistent updates for scientific predictors such as GEARS, PhaseNet and ClimaX. The LLM writes update programs and small numerical solvers fit tiny editable pools. Relative improvements range from 5.10\% to 18.56\% against an Adam baseline matched on the same objectives \citep{ax2611_07319}. HypoLoop, from the same line, lets the LLM choose \emph{where} to edit and \emph{what} to fit, while numerical solvers write the values \citep{ax2611_23718}.
\item \textbf{Aggregation and training.} FedReason applies the idea to federated aggregation, where proxy-evaluation feedback mattered more than weight access in ablations \citep{ax2611_08383}. GradReason lets an LLM write weight-update programs inside a training run. Its savings lie on the recipe's own data-accuracy frontier \citep{ax2611_11005}.
\item \textbf{Null and protocol results.} A careful study of LLM-guided gradient-free editing of YOLO detectors found that development-set gains largely did not transfer to a disjoint selection set \citep{ax2611_17789}. A perceptual super-resolution study is mostly a protocol, and its executed feedback improvements did not generalize \citep{ax2611_18690}. A position paper conjectures, without experiments, that a recursive loop could improve image-restoration diffusion models \citep{ax2611_04268}.
\item \textbf{Systems for governed loops.} W-ISA executes weight-intervention programs transactionally and reversibly \citep{ax2611_13232}. NCP versions and admits temporary interventions behind a verifier gate; in its only recorded cycle, all candidates were archived \citep{ax2611_13236}. A governed self-improvement ladder for a local security analyzer places weight editing as the most expensive rung, to be used only after prompt and LoRA changes fail \citep{ax2611_10425}.
\item \textbf{Physical parameters.} Coding agents trained optical neural networks in situ by probing hardware and writing phase masks \citep{ax2611_23875}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{B5: Efficient and non-LLM solvers}
\label{sec:solvers}

The LLM editor is expensive, and a clear trend in the corpus is to replace it once a working edit is known.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Tree-Ising} recasts the Qwen loop repair as row-wise Ising problems over BF16 sign and exponent bits. It reaches 54.6\% (annealing) and 55.1\% (simulated bifurcation) on MATH-500, against 51.7\% for the agent's edit under the same protocol. The recipe runs in 31 minutes for about \$0.90, which the authors compare with \$312 for the agent run (see \cref{sec:cost} for how that figure was derived) \citep{ax2611_14210}.
\item \textbf{Just sort it.} A short note shows that the linear program behind a solver-based anti-loop edit can be replaced by sorting neurons by coefficient of variation. The sorted edit scores 67.0\% against 67.4\% for the LP edit on 1{,}000 problems \citep{ax2611_21432}.
\item \textbf{Scripted pipelines.} The same review reports a third-party scripted pipeline with no LLM that re-derived a similar edit in about five CPU minutes, reaching roughly 75\% of the loop suppression \citep{zen23954378}.
\item \textbf{Object detectors.} A detector study found that a vanilla WSP procedure degrades 13 of 17 converged detectors, while a reparameterized closed-form variant gives positive gains on all of them \citep{ax2611_29882}.
\item \textbf{A gradient baseline for the founding edit.} Sparse fine-tuning of the 140 to 139{,}750 highest-gradient weights of the same Qwen model raises thinking-mode MATH-500 at a 4{,}096-token budget from 15.4\% to 39.0--45.5\%, in 2 to 9 minutes on one GPU. Its edits act as constant vectors added to the residual stream (\cref{sec:baselines}) \citep{ax2611_33133}.
\item \textbf{Closed-form constructions described as WSP.} ZeGA adds gated channels to existing models in closed form and presents itself as an extension of the original post. It raised a Swedish benchmark for an 8B model from 73.67\% to 84.00\%, with detachable edits that restore the base exactly \citep{zen24168084}. GUI-WEdit constructs a guarded closed-form update to one MLP tensor of a 4B GUI agent from trajectory feedback (0.535\% of parameters). It reports 48.02\% on OSWorld, against a historical 43.43\% for the base, with no other baseline \citep{ax2611_32332}. A third report uses the WSP label for refusal-direction projection and model grafting, and gives only throughput figures and three example prompts \citep{zen23958069}.
\item \textbf{Theory.} Two monographs formalize WSP editing as constrained optimization with exact preservation contracts \citep{zen23933590}, and as an inverse problem with a constructive exact ReLU compiler for finite contracts \citep{zen24198536}.
\item \textbf{Other directions.} Owned, withdrawable edits to a compiled rule network \citep{ax2611_15917}. Training-free ``weight baking'' by small open-model analysts using closed-form ridge solves \citep{ax2611_21310}. Training target networks to be easier for LLM editors to edit \citep{ax2611_26408}.
\end{itemize}
The common lesson is that LLM sessions are increasingly used as a \emph{discovery} step: they find which direction or structure to change, and a cheap procedure then reproduces or replaces the edit \citep{ax2611_14210,ax2611_31233,ax2611_28105}.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

Reported costs span four orders of magnitude.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Cheap runs.} A full set of 10-, 25- and 50-pass Qwen replication runs cost \$8.48 in API fees \citep{ax2611_20953}. Backdoor implantation cost \$1.74 per run \citep{ax2611_27920}, and federated aggregation cost \$0.79 per 60-round run \citep{ax2611_08383}.
\item \textbf{Typical agent sessions.} A Keel editing run averaged 104 editor turns, 3.9M input tokens (mostly cached) and 168k output tokens \citep{ax2611_25257}. Few-shot head edits cost \$1.59 to \$4.57 per task \citep{ax2611_28105}. All tool-free quantization experiments together would cost about \$81 at list prices \citep{ax2611_33031}. Agentic editing of a production recommender took 24.6\% of the GPU hours of a fine-tune \citep{ax2611_29613}.
\item \textbf{Expensive studies.} The interpretable-WSP study cost about \$17{,}500 at list prices including exploration \citep{ax2611_18114}. The largest writing study used 588M input and 209M output tokens \citep{ax2611_16981}.
\item \textbf{Wall-clock time.} Wall-clock times range from minutes to the original 50-pass run's 20.3 hours, which included a 4.7-hour API outage, as recorded in the released logs \citep{takehi2026sparks}.
\item \textbf{Against gradient descent.} The same small networks are often trainable by gradient descent in seconds. For example, the EEG writing task took 38--39 minutes of LLM time against 2--5 seconds of GPU training \citep{ax2611_13758}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{How the 89.7M-token figure was derived.} A figure of 89.7M tokens and 15.6 hours for the original edit comes from Tree-Ising's reconstruction from the released logs \citep{ax2611_14210}. We recomputed it from the same logs (Appendix~\ref{app:method}). Summing per-turn usage over the 158 turns of the 50-pass run reproduces 89.69M tokens. However, the logs' README states that per-turn usage ``accumulates over a conversation, so do not sum it over turns''. Counting each of the four conversations once gives about 5.6M tokens. One conversation's counter stalls after a harness restart, and a count that allows for this gives about 6.5M. Either way, this is roughly an order of magnitude below 89.7M. The 15.6-hour figure is consistent with the logs: 20.3 hours of wall-clock time minus the outage, including all forward passes. Cost comparisons between agent and solver edits should be read with this correction in mind, and future work should report editor tokens per conversation rather than summed per turn.
